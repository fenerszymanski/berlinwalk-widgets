## Prologue: Molkenmarkt today · 2019-present

**Claim.** Berlin's state archaeologists have investigated the future Molkenmarkt development area since 2019. The authority describes Molkenmarkt as Berlin's oldest market and an archive of roughly 800 years of urban history.

**Public copy, 78 words.**

> At Molkenmarkt, Berlin is not finished with its own beginning. Since 2019, state archaeologists have been examining the ground before a new quarter is built here. They call this Berlin's oldest market and an archive of around 800 years of city life. That is a useful way to start: the streets under your feet are not a backdrop. They are evidence. Look for the low ground by the Spree, then imagine traders, bridges and courts pressing close together.

Sources: [Landesdenkmalamt excavation page](https://www.berlin.de/landesdenkmalamt/archaeologie/bodendenkmalpflege/grabungen/grabung-am-molkenmarkt/), [project FAQ](https://www.berlin.de/landesdenkmalamt/archaeologie/bodendenkmalpflege/grabungen/grabung-am-molkenmarkt/faq/).

## Two towns on the Spree · 1237-1244

**Claim.** Cölln and Berlin developed as merchant settlements on opposite Spree banks. Cölln's first written record is 1237; Berlin's is 1244.

**Public copy, 77 words.**

> The year 1237 belongs to Cölln, not to a finished city called Berlin. Cölln and Berlin grew as two merchant settlements on opposite banks of the Spree. Cölln appears in writing in 1237; Berlin follows in 1244. The river was a working route, a crossing and a boundary all at once. Stand near the water and read the city as a pair, not a single founding myth. The first documents are milestones, not a certain birthday party.

Sources: [Berlin.de, Medieval trading centre](https://www.berlin.de/en/history/8476760-8619314-the-medieval-trading-center.en.html), [PETRI Berlin](https://www.berlin.de/landesdenkmalamt/archaeologie/petri-berlin/).

## United, then overruled · 1307-1448

**Claim.** In 1307 Berlin and Cölln formed an outward-facing union with a shared council while retaining separate administrations and finances. The arrangement tightened in 1432. In 1442 the elector dissolved the common government and claimed space in Cölln for a castle; the 1448 Berliner Unwille flooded the building site but failed to restore the towns' autonomy.

**Public copy, 65 words.**

> Berlin and Cölln joined forces in 1307, sharing a council and speaking outwardly as one while keeping separate administrations. Their union tightened in 1432. Ten years later, Elector Friedrich II dissolved the common government and claimed space in Cölln for a castle. In the 1448 “Berliner Unwille,” citizens flooded its building site, but failed to restore their autonomy. Power was moving toward the Hohenzollern court.

Sources: [Berlin.de, Medieval trading centre](https://www.berlin.de/geschichte/8476760-3689745-die-mittelalterliche-handelsstadt.html), [Berlin.de, The electors' residence](https://www.berlin.de/geschichte/8477039-3689745-die-kurfuerstliche-residenzstadt.html), [Stiftung Stadtmuseum Berlin, BerlinZEIT press dossier](https://www.stadtmuseum.de/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Pressemappe_BerlinZEIT.pdf), [Stiftung Stadtmuseum Berlin, A walk through Baroque Berlin](https://staging.stadtmuseum.de/story/ein-spaziergang-durchs-barocke-berlin).

## War, refuge, rebuilding · 1618-1688

**Claim.** The Thirty Years' War left Berlin-Cölln diminished and damaged. From 1658 a star-shaped fortress and extensions reshaped the city. The 1685 Edict of Potsdam brought French Protestant Huguenots alongside other newcomers. Berlin's population was about 20,000 by 1688. A precise 1648 population is intentionally omitted because the cited official sources disagree.

**Public copy, 64 words.**

> Thirty years of war left Berlin-Cölln diminished and damaged. From 1658, a star-shaped fortress and planned extensions began reshaping the medieval twin town. After the Edict of Potsdam in 1685, French Protestant refugees, the Huguenots, joined other newcomers, bringing skills, businesses and new communities. By 1688, the population had reached about 20,000. Look for a surviving piece of the old fortification near Märkisches Museum.

Sources: [Berlin.de, The electors' residence](https://www.berlin.de/geschichte/8477039-3689745-die-kurfuerstliche-residenzstadt.html), [Berlin.de, The electors' residence, English](https://www.berlin.de/en/history/8477039-8619314-the-electors-residence.en.html), [Stiftung Stadtmuseum Berlin, BerlinZEIT press dossier](https://www.stadtmuseum.de/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Pressemappe_BerlinZEIT.pdf), [Stiftung Stadtmuseum Berlin, A walk through Baroque Berlin](https://staging.stadtmuseum.de/story/ein-spaziergang-durchs-barocke-berlin).

## A royal capital takes shape · 1701-1740

**Claim.** In 1701 Friedrich III became Friedrich I, King in Prussia, and Berlin became the royal residence. Friedrichstadt's rectilinear plan began in 1688 and expanded south in 1732.

**Public copy, 79 words.**

> Power changed the scale of Berlin. In 1701 Friedrich III became Friedrich I, King in Prussia, and Berlin became the royal residence. The city did not suddenly gain its neat streets that year: the rectilinear plan of Friedrichstadt had begun in 1688 and was extended south in 1732. That distinction matters. A ruler can accelerate a city without inventing every line on its map. Around Gendarmenmarkt and Friedrichstraße, look for the long straight decisions that still organise the walk.

Sources: [Berlin.de, Royal capital](https://www.berlin.de/en/history/8477225-8619314-the-royal-capital.en.html), [Berlin Senate, Friedrichstadt history](https://www.berlin.de/sen/stadtentwicklung/staedtebau/einzelprojekte/checkpoint-charlie/).

## The dense industrial city · 1871-1900

**Claim.** The 1862 Hobrecht Plan set a street and block framework. From about 1870, parceling and dense development created the Mietskaserne courtyard form.

**Public copy, 84 words.**

> By the late nineteenth century, Berlin was growing faster than its old scale could hold. The Hobrecht Plan of 1862 laid out a framework of streets and blocks. From around 1870, owners and builders divided those blocks into deep plots and dense courtyard housing, the Mietskasernen. The plan did not build the tenements by itself. It created a frame that later investment filled hard. When you see a sequence of courtyards in Kreuzberg or Wedding, notice how a city plan became an everyday address.

Sources: [Berlin Senate, Dragonerareal history](https://www.berlin.de/rathausblock-fk/gebiet/geschichte-des-dragonerareals/), [Berlin Environmental Atlas, urban structure](https://www.berlin.de/umweltatlas/en/land-use/urban-structure/2020/introduction/).

## Greater Berlin · 1920

**Claim.** On 1 October 1920, Old Berlin, seven neighbouring municipal cities, 59 rural communities and 27 estate districts became Greater Berlin. Its population was about 3.8 million and its area expanded from 65.72 to 878.1 square kilometres.

**Public copy, 86 words.**

> On 1 October 1920, Berlin stopped being only its old core. Old Berlin joined seven neighbouring municipal cities, 59 rural communities and 27 estate districts to form Greater Berlin. The population rose to about 3.8 million, while the city area expanded from 65.72 to 878.1 square kilometres. That is why Berlin can feel like several cities stitched together: it was deliberately assembled that way. The new city began with 20 boroughs, not today's 12. Its size was a political decision before it was a visitor's map.

Sources: [Berlin.de, Greater Berlin Act](https://www.berlin.de/en/history/8481401-8619314-greater-berlin-act.en.html), [Berlin registry office history](https://www.berlin.de/standesamt/urkunden/artikel.1433627.php).

## Dictatorship, deportation and destruction · 1933-1945

**Claim.** The Nazi state persecuted Berlin Jews, stripped them of rights and property, and began systematic deportations in October 1941. A Berlin memorial records that more than 50,000 Berlin Jews were deported and murdered between October 1941 and February 1945.

**Public copy, 83 words.**

> From 1933, the Nazi state turned Berlin into a capital of persecution. Berlin Jews were stripped of rights and property; systematic deportations began in October 1941. The memorial at Gleis 17 records more than 50,000 Berlin Jews deported and murdered between October 1941 and February 1945. This cannot be reduced to an atmospheric chapter in a city story. Name the perpetrators, pause at the evidence, and let the dates stay precise. By 1945, bombing and battle had also left much of Berlin destroyed.

Sources: [Gleis 17 memorial background](https://www.berlin.de/ba-charlottenburg-wilmersdorf/ueber-den-bezirk/geschichte/gedenkstaetten/artikel.1137369.php), [United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, Berlin](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/berlin), [Berlin.de, Berlin after 1945](https://www.berlin.de/en/history/8481782-8619314-berlin-after-1945.en.html).

## Four sectors and the Airlift · 1945-1949

**Claim.** Post-war Berlin was divided into four sectors. During the 1948 blockade, Western Allies supplied West Berlin by air; the blockade was lifted on 12 May 1949, while the airlift continued into autumn.

**Public copy, 89 words.**

> After the war, Berlin was divided into four sectors. That is different from Germany's four occupation zones, and the distinction matters on every map. In June 1948, Soviet authorities blocked land, rail and water access to West Berlin. The Western Allies supplied the city by air. The blockade was lifted on 12 May 1949, but the Airlift continued into the autumn. Tempelhof, Tegel and Gatow became more than airports: they were working entries to an enclosed city. Follow the arrows as a supply story, not a decorative flight path.

Sources: [AlliiertenMuseum, Berlin Airlift](https://www.alliiertenmuseum.de/en/thema/the-berlin-airlift-1948-49/), [US Office of the Historian](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1945-1952/berlin-airlift).

## The Wall · 1961-1989

**Claim.** GDR forces began sealing the sector border with barbed wire on the night of 12-13 August 1961. Crossings opened on 9 November 1989; most physical removal followed in 1990.

**Public copy, 84 words.**

> On the night of 12-13 August 1961, GDR forces began sealing Berlin's sector border with barbed wire. The barriers became a fortified Wall system that shaped ordinary journeys for almost three decades. On 9 November 1989, crossings opened after Günter Schabowski's confused announcement and the pressure at Bornholmer Straße. That was not German reunification, which came on 3 October 1990. Nor did every concrete section vanish overnight. Use the Berlin Wall Timeline for the fuller map and sequence; this story only marks the hinge.

Sources: [Berlin Wall construction and development](https://www.berlin.de/landesdenkmalamt/denkmale/highlight-berliner-mauer/aufbau-und-entwicklung/building-and-development-of-the-berlin-wall-648927.php), [Berlin.de, opening and fall](https://www.berlin.de/en/history/8482274-8619314-opening-and-fall-of-the-berlin-wall.en.html).

## A reunited capital · 1990-2016

**Claim.** German reunification took effect on 3 October 1990. On 20 June 1991, the Bundestag voted to move parliament and government to Berlin. Potsdamer and Leipziger Platz became a new urban district after the Wall period.

**Public copy, 69 words.**

> Reunification took effect on 3 October 1990. On 20 June 1991, the Bundestag separately voted to move parliament and government to Berlin. Potsdamer and Leipziger Platz show the long rebuilding process at full scale: a former border void became a dense district of offices, stations and public space. Compare the new skyline with the missing streets beneath it; this dramatic rebuild is only one layer of the reunited city.

Sources: [German Bundestag, Bonn upon Spree](https://www.bundestag.de/en/visittheBundestag/exhibitions/bonn-upon-spree-1013402), [Berlin.de, The New Berlin](https://www.berlin.de/en/history/8482304-8619314-the-new-berlin.en.html), [Leipziger Platz memorial context](https://www.berlin.de/landesdenkmalamt/denkmale/highlight-berliner-mauer/mauer-denkmale/leipziger-platz-648152.php).

## Epilogue: Four places to read Berlin today

**Claim.** Molkenmarkt, Friedrichstadt, Gleis 17 and Potsdamer/Leipziger Platz can be used as a deliberately limited present-day reading list for Berlin history.

**Public copy, 63 words.**

> These four places are separate starting points, not one walking route: Molkenmarkt for the buried market city; Friedrichstraße and Gendarmenmarkt for the planned royal capital; Gleis 17 at S Grunewald for the evidence of deportation; Potsdamer and Leipziger Platz for the post-Wall rebuild. Start with one place and one date, then notice what has changed, what survives and what the map leaves out.

## Editorial and safety constraints

- Do not state that 1237 is Berlin's certain founding date.
- Keep 1307 as an outward-facing union and shared council with separate administrations and finances; do not call it a full merger.
- Keep the 1432 tightening, the 1442 dissolution of the common government and the Cölln castle-site claim distinct from the 1448 Berliner Unwille.
- Do not imply permanent electoral residence before 1486.
- Do not use a precise 1648 population figure; official sources disagree. Use diminished and damaged, then the sourced about-20,000 figure for 1688.
- Do not say that Huguenots rebuilt Berlin on their own; present them alongside other newcomers and within a wider political rebuilding programme.
- Use **King in Prussia**, not King of Prussia.
- Do not make the Hobrecht Plan the sole actor in constructing tenement housing.
- Do not combine the two different deportation denominators in the source record.
- Do not collapse 9 November 1989, 3 October 1990 and 20 June 1991 into one event.
- Do not place a booking or sales CTA in the dictatorship, deportation and destruction chapter.
- The closing CTA may describe the Free Tour only as a 2-hour walk starting at Alexanderplatz, covering the historic centre of former East Berlin. It must say that it does not follow the Wall line.
