Most people walk up to the Reichstag, photograph the glass dome, and move on to the Brandenburg Gate a few minutes away. I understand why. From the front it looks like one solid, slightly stern old parliament with a modern hat on top. What that first glance hides is that you are looking at the single most rebuilt, refought-over building in Germany, a place that has been an imperial showpiece, a burnt-out shell, a battlefield, a Cold War dead end, and finally a working democracy, all on the same patch of ground.

That is what makes the Reichstag worth more than a photo. Seen before and after, it is a short history of the whole German 20th century written in one facade. My advice: give it ten minutes and learn what you are actually looking at, because once you can read the layers, this becomes one of the most moving stops in the city. Here is the building, then and now, and how to get inside it for free.

## One building, five different Germanys

The reason the Reichstag rewards a close look is that nothing about it was ever left alone. Most famous buildings tell one story. This one was built by an empire, gutted by fire, fought over street by street, left to rot beside the Berlin Wall, and then rebuilt as the home of a reunified democracy. The stone in front of you carries all of that at once.

So instead of treating it as one building, it helps to read it as a stack of layers, oldest at the bottom. Once you know the layers are there, you start spotting them: a scar in the stone, four bronze words, a glass dome that only makes sense as an answer to everything below it.

## Before: an empire built it to look powerful

The Reichstag went up between 1884 and 1894 to plans by the architect Paul Wallot, and Kaiser Wilhelm II laid the final stone on 5 December 1894. The young German Empire wanted a parliament building that looked as heavy and permanent as the monarchies around it, so Wallot gave it thick stone walls, four corner towers, and a great dome that was, for its day, an engineering showpiece.

There is a small irony built into the front. The bronze words above the main entrance, **Dem Deutschen Volke** (**To the German People**), were only fixed in place in **1916**, more than twenty years after the building opened. The space had been left empty that whole time because dedicating the house to the people, rather than to the Kaiser, was politically awkward. The lettering you see was designed in 1916 by Peter Behrens.

## The fire that changed everything

On the night of **27 February 1933**, the Reichstag burned. The plenary chamber and the original dome were gutted. Hitler had been chancellor for just four weeks, and he used the fire to push through the Reichstag Fire Decree, which suspended civil liberties overnight and became one of the first concrete steps from democracy into dictatorship.

This is the strange thing to stand there and realise: **the fire left almost nothing you can point to today**. The burned-out original dome was taken down years later, so the dome above you now is not the one that burned. What the fire really destroyed was not stone. It was German democracy, for the next twelve years.

## After the war: a ruin on the wrong side of the Wall

By 1945 the Reichstag was barely used, but the Red Army made it the symbolic prize of the Battle of Berlin. Soviet soldiers raised a flag over the ruin, an image staged for Yevgeny Khaldei's famous photograph on 2 May 1945, and scrawled their names in Cyrillic across the inner walls. If you look closely at the lower stonework today you can still find **shrapnel and bullet scars**, and inside, on a dome visit, that **Soviet graffiti was deliberately kept** rather than cleaned off when the building was rebuilt in the 1990s.

Then came the quiet decades. The shell sat in West Berlin with its dome gone, patched up in a plain 1961 to 1971 restoration and left flat-roofed and half-empty, because the real parliament met in Bonn and the **Berlin Wall ran directly behind the building**. For thirty years this was a monument on a dead-end street. The one great moment came in the summer of 1995, when the artists Christo and Jeanne-Claude [wrapped the entire Reichstag in silver fabric](https://christojeanneclaude.net/artworks/wrapped-reichstag/) for two weeks and about five million people came to see it, just before the rebuild finally began.

## After: a democracy you can walk over

The Reichstag you actually visit is Norman Foster's, rebuilt between **1994 and 1999**. His design kept the old scarred outer walls, graffiti and all, but gutted the inside and crowned it with a walk-up **glass dome** sitting directly over the debating chamber. The German parliament, the Bundestag, sat here again for the first time on **19 April 1999**.

The dome is not decoration. Standing in clear glass above the room where laws are made, with a mirrored cone bouncing daylight down into the chamber, it is a deliberate statement: the citizens are physically above their politicians, and they can watch. Walk up the spiral ramp and you also get one of the best free views in Berlin, straight across the government quarter, the Tiergarten and the Brandenburg Gate.

## How to actually get inside

The dome and roof terrace are **free**, but you cannot just walk up. You have to **register in advance** through the official [German Bundestag visitor service](https://www.bundestag.de/en/visittheBundestag), giving the full name and date of birth of everyone in your group. On the day, each visitor aged 16 or over shows the **passport or ID card** they registered with, so bring it.

A few practical notes so a great free visit does not fall apart at the door:

- **Book as early as you can.** Slots open a few months ahead and the popular late-afternoon and evening times fill fast in summer.
- **The dome is open daily from 8:00 until midnight,** with last admission at 21:45. Going near sunset gives you the view in daylight and the city lighting up as you come down.
- **No booking? Try the Visitors' Service centre** in the small pavilion just south of the building. It can issue same-day or next-few-days passes, but it must be at least two hours before your visit and it sells out on busy days.
- **Bring the exact ID you registered.** A name that does not match the passport is the most common reason people get turned away.
