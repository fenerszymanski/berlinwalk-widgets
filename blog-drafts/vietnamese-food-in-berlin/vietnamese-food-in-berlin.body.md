Ask ten guidebooks what to eat in Berlin and you will get the same two answers: currywurst and doner. Both are worth trying, and I send guests to my favourite [doner counters](/post/how-to-order-doner-in-berlin) all the time. But the food a lot of Berliners quietly reach for on a normal weeknight is neither of those. It is Vietnamese. Berlin has one of the largest and best Vietnamese food scenes in Europe, and most visitors walk straight past it because nobody told them it was here.

The reason it is so good is a real piece of Berlin history, not a marketing story. And once you understand the shape of it, the city's hundreds of Vietnamese kitchens stop feeling random and start making sense.

## Why Berlin has such good Vietnamese food

This is one of those Berlin things where the food and the history are the same story. The city is home to around 40,000 people of Vietnamese background, the largest Southeast Asian community in Germany, and they arrived here in two very different ways, on two different sides of the Wall.

In the 1980s, socialist East Germany signed a labour agreement with North Vietnam and brought in tens of thousands of Vietnamese contract workers. They were housed near the factories in the eastern districts, above all in **Lichtenberg and Marzahn**, and after 1989 many stayed and built small businesses. In the West, a separate group had already arrived in the late 1970s: refugees from South Vietnam, the so-called boat people, who were resettled across West Berlin.

Two arrivals, two halves of the city, one food culture that outlasted the country that first invited it. If you have ever bought a late snack from a Vietnamese-run [Spaeti](/post/what-is-a-spati-berlin), you have already met a small piece of this history without noticing.

## The three worlds of Vietnamese Berlin

Here is the mental map I give guests. Vietnamese food in Berlin lives in three worlds, and knowing which one you want saves you a lot of aimless scrolling.

### Central classics in Mitte

If you are staying near the middle and want a genuinely good bowl without a detour, Mitte is your answer. **Monsieur Vuong** on Alte Schoenhauser Strasse has been running since 1999 and is close to the oldest Vietnamese restaurant still going in the city. The menu is deliberately small and changes often, the curries are fragrant, and there are no reservations, so at busy times you wait a little. A few minutes away, **District Mot** on Rosenthaler Strasse does a louder, plastic-stool Saigon street-food act that is good fun with a group.

Both are walkable from the eastern end of my [free walking tour](/book-berlin-walking-tour/berlin-free-walking-tour-tip-based) route, which is why I know them well.

![A bowl of beef pho, the Vietnamese noodle soup you will find on almost every Berlin Vietnamese menu](blog-drafts/vietnamese-food-in-berlin/images/opt/01-pho-cover.jpg)

_Pho, the clear beef or chicken noodle soup, is the dish to order first if you are new to Vietnamese food._

### Kantstrasse, the west-side food street

Cross to the western side and you find Berlin's unofficial Asian food street: **Kantstrasse** in Charlottenburg, near Savignyplatz. This is where I send people who want to sit down and eat a proper dinner rather than grab a fast bowl. **Madame Ngo** is a Hanoi-style brasserie whose pho bo is the house signature, and **Ben Thanh**, named after Saigon's market, does a well-priced crispy duck a few doors along. The whole street is worth a slow wander if you are exploring the [west of the city](/post/kreuzberg-berlin) anyway.

### Kreuzberg and the vegan side

The third world is the neighbourhood one, and Kreuzberg is its centre. This is the easiest area if you or anyone in your group is eating [vegetarian or vegan](/post/vegan-berlin-guide-2026), because Vietnamese cooking leans hard on tofu, herbs and rice. **Good Morning Vietnam** on Bergmannstrasse is fully vegan with a leafy back terrace, **Maison Umami** near the canal does gentle, lantern-lit bowls at fair prices, and **Miss Saigon** is a simple, well-loved spot with a long meat-free list. A Vietnamese dinner pairs naturally with a [Kreuzberg or Prenzlauer Berg](/post/prenzlauer-berg-berlin) evening.

## The Dong Xuan Center: the real deep dive

If you want to see where all of this comes from, ride out east. The **Dong Xuan Center** in Lichtenberg is not a restaurant. It is a Vietnamese wholesale market spread across long metal halls on Herzbergstrasse, on an old industrial site, and it is the trading heart of the community. Inside you will find hair salons, fabric stalls, importers, travel agents, and kitchens that serve some of the cheapest, most honest pho and bun in Berlin to a mostly Vietnamese crowd.

It is not polished, and that is the point. Take **tram M8 to Herzbergstrasse/Industriegebiet**, give yourself a couple of hours, and bring cash, because many stalls do not take cards. It is one of the few places in the city where you feel you have stepped fully out of the tourist Berlin and into a working one.

## What to order if the menu is new to you

Vietnamese menus in Berlin are usually friendly, with photos and English, but a few words help you order like you know what you are doing.

- **Pho** is the clear noodle soup, beef (pho bo) or chicken (pho ga). Start here. It is the dish that tells you whether a kitchen is any good.
- **Bun** is a cold rice-noodle bowl topped with grilled meat or tofu, herbs and peanuts, poured over with a light dressing. Perfect on a warm day.
- **Banh mi** is the Vietnamese baguette sandwich, a direct echo of French colonial history, crunchy and cheap and ideal for a walk.
- **Goi cuon** are the fresh, translucent summer rolls you dip in peanut sauce, lighter than the fried spring rolls.
- **Ca phe sua da** is the strong iced coffee with condensed milk. Order one and thank me later.

## A few practical notes

A handful of small things will make the meal smoother:

- **Cash still matters** at the cheaper end, especially at the Dong Xuan Center and small snack bars. Carry some.
- **Tipping is light.** Rounding up or adding a little is normal, not the North American percentages. I explain the local habit in the [tipping guide](/post/tipping-in-berlin).
- **You do not need German.** Staff at the popular places speak English, and pointing at a photo works everywhere. If you want a few words anyway, the [do you need German](/post/do-i-need-to-speak-german-to-visit-berlin) post has the basics.
- **Check hours before a long trip.** For a run out to Lichtenberg especially, confirm the market and its kitchens are open before you set off.

For a wider look at eating in the city, the [what to eat in Berlin](/post/what-to-eat-in-berlin-12-must-try-local-foods) guide covers the local classics, and the [halal food](/post/halal-food-in-berlin) and [breakfast](/post/breakfast-in-berlin) guides cover two more everyday questions.

If you take one thing from this: on your first free evening, skip the third currywurst and find a bowl of pho instead. It is the meal that quietly tells you the most about how modern Berlin actually came together.
