Berlin looks, at first, like a city that keeps its best parts on the street. You walk down a long block of shopfronts and tall grey facades and assume that is all there is. It usually is not. Behind those facades, especially around Hackescher Markt, the city hides a second layer: connected courtyards, called Höfe, that most visitors walk straight past on their way to the next big sight.

My advice: do not treat these as a secret you have to unlock. The best courtyards near Hackescher Markt are free, open in the daytime, and clustered within a ten-minute walk of each other. The useful skill is knowing which passage to step into, and in what order. This is the short walk I send people on when my tour finishes and they ask what to do next.

## What Berlin courtyards (Höfe) really are

A Hof is simply a courtyard, but in Berlin the word carries a whole piece of history. When the city exploded in size in the late 1800s, developers built deep. Instead of one building on the street, they filled the block behind it with a chain of inner courtyards, sometimes five or six deep, stacked with workshops, small factories and cramped rental flats.

Most of these back courtyards were plain and functional. A handful were built, or later restored, to be genuinely beautiful. That is what makes the cluster around Hackescher Markt special: several of the good ones sit almost side by side, so you can walk from a polished showpiece into a raw street-art passage into a quiet rose garden in the space of a few minutes.

Two things to know before you step in. First, **the courtyards are private property that the public is allowed to walk through**, so keep your voice down near the residential ones. Second, **the residential courtyards are calmest in the morning**, before the shops and tour groups arrive.

## The walk at a glance

This is a short loop, not a hike. From **S Hackescher Markt** to the **Heckmann-Höfe** is roughly 1.2 kilometres of actual walking, spread across five courtyard stops. You could rush it in half an hour, but the point is to slow down, so give it a relaxed morning or an easy afternoon.

## 1. Hackesche Höfe: the famous one

Start where almost everyone does. The [Hackesche Höfe](https://www.hackesche-hoefe.de/en) are eight interconnected courtyards at Rosenthaler Straße 40, opened in 1906 and the largest single courtyard complex in Germany. Walk in through the main arch and the first courtyard hits you immediately: a facade of blue and cream glazed brick, designed by the architect August Endell in full Art Nouveau style, wrapped around the entrance to the Chamäleon variety theatre and a small cinema.

Do not stop at the first courtyard. **Keep walking deeper into the block.** Each courtyard is numbered on the wall, Hof I through Hof VIII, and the mood changes as you go: cultural and commercial at the front, quieter and residential toward the back. The buildings were nationalised and left to decay in the GDR years, then restored from 1993 at enormous cost, which is why they look this sharp today.

## 2. Haus Schwarzenberg: the opposite, one door away

Step back onto Rosenthaler Straße, look one door south to number 39, and you find the exact opposite of the polished Höfe. [Haus Schwarzenberg](https://haus-schwarzenberg.org/en/das-haus/) was kept deliberately unrestored by an artists' association, and its narrow passage is coated top to bottom in paste-ups, stickers and spray paint that changes month to month. After the tidy Hackesche Höfe, the contrast is the point.

It is not only a photo wall. The same building holds two serious museums: the [Anne Frank Zentrum](https://www.annefrank.de/en/exhibition-berlin/your-visit/), and the Museum Otto Weidt's Workshop for the Blind, which tells how a small brush-and-broom workshop hid its Jewish employees during the war. **You can walk the passage for free**, and the museums are a short, powerful stop if you have the time. I have sent people in for the street art who came out having spent an hour with the history.

## 3. Rosenhöfe: roses and a tower over the roof

A few steps further up Rosenthaler Straße, another entrance leads into the Rosenhöfe. A 1990s redesign wrapped this courtyard in green wrought-iron balconies and planted it as a rose garden that climbs the walls through the summer. It is small and easy to miss, which is exactly why it stays calm.

Stand in the middle and look up. **On a clear day the TV Tower shows over the roofline**, one of those quiet Berlin views you only get by stepping off the street. The passage carries you through toward Sophienstraße and the quieter half of the walk.

## 4. Sophienstraße and the quiet Höfe

Come out near Sophienstraße and the tempo drops completely. This is one of the few streets in Mitte that still looks close to its 18th and 19th-century self, restored rather than flattened and rebuilt. Duck into the passages at numbers 19 to 21 and the street noise disappears.

The **Sophie-Gips-Höfe**, a former sewing-machine factory, is the elegant one here: brick, ivy and a gallery-quiet feel a couple of minutes from the crowds. This is the stretch where the walk stops feeling like sightseeing and starts feeling like ordinary Berlin, which is the best thing these courtyards do.

## When to go, and how to walk it well

A few practical notes so the walk actually works:

- **Go in the morning if you want it quiet.** By early afternoon the Hackesche Höfe fill with tour groups and shoppers. The residential courtyards never get truly busy, but they feel best early.
- **The courtyards are free; the businesses inside keep their own hours.** Shops and cafés open late morning and close in the early evening, so a mid-morning to late-afternoon window catches everything open.
- **If you want the candy workshop, plan around it.** The Bonbonmacherei in the Heckmann-Höfe, where sweets are still boiled and pulled by hand, only opens a few days a week and usually takes a summer break. Check before you make a special trip. The [Heckmann-Höfe](https://www.visitberlin.de/en/candy-making-heckmannhofe-bonbonmacherei) courtyard itself stays open either way.
- **Getting there is easy.** S Hackescher Markt is served by the S3, S5, S7 and S9, plus the M1, M4, M5 and M6 trams. There is no U-Bahn at Hackescher Markt itself; the nearest is Weinmeisterstraße on the U8, about five minutes away.
