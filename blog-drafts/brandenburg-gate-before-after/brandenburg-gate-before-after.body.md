Most people photograph the Brandenburg Gate in about fifteen seconds and walk on. I understand the instinct. It sits there on Pariser Platz looking calm, floodlit and permanent, like it has always been exactly this. The truth is almost the opposite. This is the survivor. Napoleon stole its statue, bombs left it standing alone in a field of rubble, and for nearly thirty years it was locked inside no-man's-land where nobody could reach it. Learning the Brandenburg Gate before and after those moments is what turns a quick photo stop into the one place in Berlin where you can actually feel the whole century in your feet.

## Before: a grand gate for a small kingdom

The Gate you see was built between **1788 and 1791** for King Friedrich Wilhelm II, designed by Carl Gotthard Langhans and modelled on the gateway to the Acropolis in Athens. Berlin once had eighteen city gates in its customs wall. This is the only one left standing, which is part of why it carries so much weight now.

Two years later the sculptor Johann Gottfried Schadow added the **Quadriga** on top: a chariot pulled by four horses and driven by a winged goddess. Look up and notice which way she faces. She looks **east, down Unter den Linden into the old heart of the city**, not out toward the Tiergarten. That small detail tells you the Gate was always meant to be an entrance into Berlin, not an exit from it. One more thing worth knowing as you pass through: the wide central passage was originally reserved for the royal family. Ordinary Berliners used the side arches.

## The Quadriga's round trip to Paris

In **1806** Napoleon marched into Berlin, and he liked the Quadriga enough to take it. The whole chariot was crated up and carried off to Paris as a war trophy. For eight years the Gate stood bare-topped, a public reminder that Prussia had lost.

When the Prussians reached Paris in **1814** they took it straight back. On its return the architect Karl Friedrich Schinkel added an **iron cross and a Prussian eagle** to the goddess's staff, quietly turning her into a figure of victory. That is the version standing up there today, which means the statue you are looking at has already done a return trip across Europe and come home.

## After the war: the only thing left standing

Skip forward to **1945**. The bombing raids and the final Battle of Berlin turned Pariser Platz into rubble. The hotels, the embassies, the Adlon, all of it gone or gutted. The Brandenburg Gate was badly damaged and shell-pocked, but it stayed up, and for a while it was **the only recognisable structure left in the square**. Soviet and Allied soldiers posed under it. The Quadriga was wrecked, and only a single horse's head survived, which you can still see today in the Märkisches Museum.

What happened next is a small miracle of a divided city cooperating. In **1956 to 1958**, East and West Berlin restored the Gate together and recast the Quadriga from old moulds, even though the Cold War was already freezing everything else between them.

## Walled off: thirty years in the death strip

Then came the hardest chapter. When the **Berlin Wall** went up in **1961**, the border ran right past the Gate, and it ended up stranded inside the death strip, the closed no-man's-land between East and West. For nearly thirty years it belonged to neither side and nobody could walk up to it. It became the single clearest image of a divided Germany, which is exactly why US President Ronald Reagan chose to stand near it in **1987** and say the line, "tear down this wall."

![The Brandenburg Gate closed off behind the barrier from the eastern side in 1982](blog-drafts/brandenburg-gate-before-after/images/opt/03_wall_1982_pd.jpg)
_1982, from the eastern side: a barrier, empty ground, and a sign warning that beyond it lay a prohibited zone._

The Wall fell on the night of 9 November 1989, but the Gate itself stayed shut a little longer. It finally **reopened on 22 December 1989**, when the two German leaders walked through it together in front of an enormous crowd. If you have seen one photo of German reunification, it is probably this spot. For the wider route of what divided the city and what is left of it, the [Berlin Wall trail guide](/post/the-berlin-wall-where-it-stood-and-what-s-left-in-2026) is a good next read.

## What you can still read on the Gate today

This is the part I care about most as a guide, because the whole story is written into the stone if you slow down for five minutes.

- **The columns.** Look closely at the sandstone on the Pariser Platz side. Some of it still carries filled and patched scars from shrapnel and gunfire in 1945.
- **The cobblestone line.** A double row of cobblestones set into the road near the Gate marks the exact line the Berlin Wall once followed across the city. Stand with one foot on each side and you are standing where East met West.
- **The Room of Silence.** Inside the northern gatehouse there is a small, free **Raum der Stille**, a quiet room open to anyone who wants a moment out of the crowd.

The Gate itself is a **free, open public square with no ticket and no queue**, accessible at any hour. That is unusual for a landmark this famous, so there is no reason to rush it. For opening details and the surrounding square, [visitBerlin](https://www.visitberlin.de/en/brandenburg-gate) and the [official Berlin.de page](https://www.berlin.de/en/attractions-and-sights/3560266-3104052-brandenburg-gate.en.html) both keep current information. If you want the practical visiting angle rather than the history, my [Brandenburg Gate visitors guide](/post/brandenburg-gate-berlin-visitors-guide) covers timing, crowds and photos.

## Where it fits on a Berlin walk

Getting here is easy. **Brandenburger Tor station sits directly underneath**, served by the S-Bahn and the U5, and on foot the Gate is simply the far end of Unter den Linden, a straight walk down from Museum Island and the TV Tower. Come from that direction and the Gate is the last thing before the green of the Tiergarten opens up.

My own tour starts a little east of here, at Alexanderplatz, for reasons I explain in [why the tour starts at Alexanderplatz](/post/why-our-tour-starts-at-alexanderplatz-and-not-at-brandenburg-gate). The Gate is one of the sights I point guests toward rather than where I begin, partly because it makes far more sense once you already understand the divided city that ran through it. If you like reading a single building as a whole century, the [Reichstag before and after](/post/reichstag-before-and-after) sits a five-minute walk to the north and tells a similar story.

My honest advice: do not treat the Brandenburg Gate as a fifteen-second photo. Walk through the side arch the way an ordinary Berliner once had to, find the cobblestone line, look up at a goddess who has already been to Paris and back, and give the survivor of Pariser Platz the couple of minutes it has earned.
